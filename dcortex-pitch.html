02The problem02 / 14

Every airline runs on a plan. The plan never survives the day.

Crew are assigned to flights weeks ahead. Then someone gets sick. A storm shuts an airport. One late aircraft breaks the four flights behind it.

A small team called Crew Control absorbs all of it. Their job is not noticing that something broke — that part is obvious. Their job is working out what breaks next, and who can legally be moved to cover it.

Today they do that by reading five or six different screens and making phone calls, under time pressure, largely from memory.

Say this "The hard part isn't the alarm. It's the answer."
03Why it's hard03 / 14

One question. Eight files. And the answer is maths, not opinion.

To say whether one person can fly one trip, you have to combine:

  • Their roster — what they are already scheduled to do
  • Their duty clock — how many hours they have already worked this week
  • Their licences and medical certificates, and when each one expires
  • Which aircraft types they are trained on
  • Where they are based, and how long it takes to reach them
  • The standby list — who is on call, and during which hours
  • And a rulebook of hard legal limits
And here is the part that makes this different

A pilot may work 60 hours in any 7 days. Covering this particular trip would put Captain C-2087 on 61 hours and 20 minutes.

That is about 2% over. In aviation, 2% over is not "close enough" — it is illegal. An answer that is nearly right is not a slightly worse answer. It is a violation.

04The obvious idea04 / 14

"Just give the AI the data and let it answer."

This is what most people try first. It works fine for simple lookups. It falls apart on exactly the questions that matter.

Where it breaks

Language models are excellent at understanding what you meant. They are unreliable at long chains of exact arithmetic — adding up seven days of hours, comparing against a limit, then checking rest gaps, then licence dates, for every candidate.

And they don't fail loudly. They produce a confident, well-written, wrong answer.

The evidence

Researchers tested today's best models on ten kinds of scheduling problem. On crew assignment — this exact problem — the models produced a workable answer 0.8% of the time.

It was the worst score of all ten. Crew assignment is the single hardest thing on that list to hand to a model and walk away from.

Say this "A confident wrong answer at 5 a.m. is worse than no answer at all — because someone acts on it."
05Our answer05 / 14

The AI does the talking. A calculator does the deciding.

We split the job in two, on purpose, and we never let the two halves swap roles.

The AI's job

Understand the question, however it is phrased. "C-1042 is sick." "My Bangalore captain on the Delhi overnight is out." "What breaks if Nair calls in?"

All three mean the same thing. The AI turns each of them into the same precise request.

Then, at the very end, it writes the answer back in plain English.

The calculator's job

Everything with a number in it. Duty hours, rest gaps, licence dates, costs — and every legal-or-illegal decision.

This is ordinary, testable code. It runs in 1.4 milliseconds, which is fast enough to check every single person on the payroll every time, instead of guessing at a shortlist.

The rule we enforce in the plumbing, not in the prompt

The AI is never shown the raw duty clocks. It only ever sees the finished numbers the calculator produced. It cannot get the arithmetic wrong, because it never sees the arithmetic.

And before any answer reaches you, we check every number in the sentence against the numbers the calculator actually produced. If one doesn't match, the answer is blocked.

08Saying no08 / 14

When we don't know, we say so — and we say what's missing

A system that answers everything is a system that makes things up. We would rather answer ten questions correctly and refuse the eleventh.

Not in the data

"Who do I actually phone?"

We say: "I don't hold crew contact details." Then we list what we do hold, so you know where to go instead. We never invent a phone number.

Not a question we should answer

"What's the chance C-2087 calls in sick tomorrow?"

We say: "That's a prediction, and we don't make predictions." We show the risk score that was handed to us, and we are clear it came from somewhere else.

Crucially, those are two different messages. "I don't have that data" and "I didn't understand you" are separate problems, and we never disguise one as the other. A tool that hides confusion behind polite humility gets caught the first time you rephrase the question.

10What we found10 / 14

Three things in this data nobody asked us to look for

Answering questions is the brief. Finding the problems before anyone asks is what makes it worth paying for.

1 · There is a trip with exactly one legal captain

Pairing P-2289 on 14 September. Out of 28 captains on the roster, exactly one could legally take it if the rostered captain called in sick — and exactly one senior cabin crew member. That is a single point of failure sitting quietly inside a published roster.

2 · The standby roster has a hole at night

Between 19:00 and 02:00 there is no reserve captain on call. None. And the only reserve captain qualified on the turboprop fleet is on call just 03:00–15:00. A sick call at 8 p.m. for an early-morning turboprop departure has no standby answer at all.

3 · One crew member is already rostered illegally

A cabin crew member's recurrent training expires on 17 September. They are scheduled to fly on 19 September. Our system finds it on startup — before anyone asks a question.

11Honest limits11 / 14

Where it breaks — and how it behaves when it does

Every system has an edge. Ours knows where its edge is, and tells you when you have reached it.

  • We can only see one week. The roster data stops on 20 September. If we are checking someone whose next trip might fall on the 21st, we cannot verify their rest. So we say "legal within the week we can see; not verifiable beyond 20 September" — rather than claiming a certainty we don't have.
  • Two problems at once, we handle. Three or more, we don't claim to. We check every pair of simultaneous disruptions and find the cheapest legal combination. Beyond two, we still return a legal plan — we just stop calling it the best one.
  • We only model crew. We don't swap aircraft, rebook passengers, or check whether a hotel room is available. Those are real parts of the job that we deliberately left out.
  • "Passengers" here means seats. The data has aircraft capacity, not actual bookings. When we say 486 passengers, we mean 486 seats — and we say so, rather than implying we know who is booked.
One more, and it is the interesting one

We found several places where the written rulebook and the actual data disagree with each other. We follow the data — but we publish a one-page report listing every disagreement, which reading we chose, and why. It ships with the code.
