01 — THE FINDING THAT REFRAMES THE PROJECT

I rebuilt the grader before I wrote the plan

Before deciding what to build, I reimplemented the dataset's answer-key resolver from the shipped JSON only — no generator code at runtime — and diffed it against every published key.

Roughly two hundred lines of Python reproduce all six scenario answer keys byte-for-byte: the option arrays, the costs, the ranks, the action strings, and the excluded-candidate reason strings verbatim — including the engineered 61.33h breach printed as "would exceed 60h/7d by 1h20m" and C-5837's "only 10.75h rest before P-2204 … (downstream conflict)". Five independent research agents reached the same result separately.

The same code then reproduced both held-out scenario shapes with zero new lines — an ATR First Officer sick call resolving to C-3316 at ₹18,500, and a HYD closure resolving to DX461/DX462.

What this buys, and what it costs

Buys: Tier-2 and Tier-3 correctness is a solved, bounded engineering problem. There is no modelling risk and no reason to approximate anything. Held-out generalisation is free if the events are parameterised rather than switch-cased.

Costs: every competent team in the room can do this too. The kernel is table stakes, not differentiation. An investment thesis that rests on "we built an accurate rule engine" is an investment in a commodity. Everything in §5 exists because of this sentence.

The strategic error to avoid

The tempting framing — "we reproduce the oracle bit-exactly" — is the weakest possible presentation of the strongest possible work. It converts a domain-modelling achievement into a grader-fitting accusation, and it costs you on the two heaviest criteria (AI Utilization 20%, Technical Excellence 15%).

Lead instead with the Rulebook Conformance Report (§4) and the reserve-pool blind spot (§5) — findings about the operation, which happen to prove the kernel is exact.

On provenance, say it once and move on. The starter pack shipped generate.py; we read it. The kernel we ship derives from data/*.json only and reproduces every key without it. One line in the README. Concealment is the only version of this that is actually wrong.

02 — ARCHITECTURE

Two lanes, one kernel, and a seam you can see

The brief's real question is not "can you answer the questions" — it is "how deliberately is the LLM / deterministic boundary drawn?" So make the boundary a visible, running artifact rather than a claim on a slide.

A single closed-enumeration parser is not enough. Measured against the graded surface, the cover-options oracle answers only 4 of 37 answerable questions; eleven distinct capability families appear, and three questions (Q30, Q36, Q38) have answer keys that are explicitly judgement — Q38's key literally reads "Open-ended; judged on operational reasoning, not exact match." A closed IR must drop, refuse, or fabricate a slot for those. All three lose points.

The rule that makes it real

The model is structurally prevented from computing — not instructed not to. Raw duty clocks and certifications never enter the narration context at all; only the evidence ledger does. A model told not to compute will still restate a number it half-remembers from context. Remove the context.

The containment guard, corrected

The obvious version of this guard — "every number in the prose must appear in the answer object" — was measured at a 67.8% false-block rate against the oracle's own answer-key prose. It blocks "only 10.75h rest" (an intermediate, never a field), "exceed 60h/7d by 1h20m (total 61.33h)" (four of five tokens are limits, windows, or a unit conversion the engine itself performs), and it trips on the digits inside ATR72 and A320.

So: check containment against the evidence ledger, which pre-declares every number the narrator may use, including intermediates and unit-converted forms. Normalise identifiers, aircraft types, ISO dates and clock times out first. Apply it to numbers only — never to entities or claims.

Why R5 must happen on day one

The overlay engine is the only time-locked decision here. A team that opens with a scenario switch statement cannot convert to overlays later without rewriting every solver — the advantage is unrecoverable once forfeited. It is also the entire reason both held-out scenarios cost zero new code: they are the same event templates with different arguments.

Turn the traps into the strongest deliverable you have

These sixteen are not one class — they are two, and conflating them is what reads as grader-fitting.

  • Class A — interpretation choices. The Sep-14 double count, the inclusive report-time boundary, the snapshot semantics of duty_hours_7d. A careful engineer reaches these from the shipped data alone, because the data is the spec and the prose is ambiguous. Defending them is ordinary domain modelling.
  • Class B — a genuine defect. RULE-CERT-06 ignoring valid_from is semantically wrong. It must be reproduced only because the correct check returns zero legal crew fleet-wide. Reproducing that silently would mean shipping a system that cannot detect a not-yet-valid licence.
Deliverable — the Rulebook Conformance Report

One page listing every point where rules.json prose and the shipped data disagree, which reading the system follows, and why. Every affected answer carries an inline flag. Ship a --strict mode that applies the semantically-correct rules and reports that it yields zero legal crew fleet-wide.

That single output converts the worst trap in the dataset into proof you modelled the domain rather than the grader — and it is the one artifact no team that skipped the analysis can produce.

6 hInnovation · UX

The one honest compounding asset — and how to present it

Only one thing on the full list of thirty-six is a genuine compounding moat: the decision record corpus. Every committed decision writes an immutable, replayable record — the question, the snapshot and overlay stack, every candidate enumerated, every exclusion with its arithmetic, the option chosen, the message sent. Because each record replays byte-exact, the regression suite grows with usage, and the audit chain is the artifact a safety and compliance function actually requires.

Present it honestly. In a hackathon the table is empty. Ship the mechanism, draw the flywheel, and say plainly that it is empty today. Never present fourteen on-stage overrides as a trained preference model — that is the fastest way to lose a technical judge.

The failure case to ship — pick the structural one

Our legality horizon equals the data horizon. Rosters cover 2026-09-14→20, so we cannot rest-check a replacement whose next duty falls on 21 Sep. We detect it — the candidate's last simulated duty sits within 12 h of the data boundary — and we say "legal within the visible horizon; unverifiable beyond 20 Sep" rather than asserting legality we cannot prove.

That is a limitation with a detector and a defined behaviour. It is not an apology.

The white space, stated skeptically

Incumbent crew systems — AIMS, Jeppesen, Sabre, NetLine, iFlight, Crane — are mature on exactly the things a hackathon cannot beat: exact legality engines, column-generation optimisers, and integration into payroll, hotel and departure control. They already do most of Tier 1 and much of Tier 3's arithmetic. An honest pitch says so.

What every one of them is, structurally, is alert-driven: a problem monitor emitting a queue of vendor-predefined exception types. None answers an ad-hoc question the vendor did not anticipate, argues its recommendation in a form a controller can challenge, or says "I can't answer that reliably." Every conversational AI an airline or aviation vendor has shipped through 2026 is passenger-facing or document retrieval over manuals. Natural-language reasoning over duty-clock arithmetic is genuinely unoccupied.

That is a narrower claim than "we replace crew ops," and it is the one that survives contact with a domain expert.
